##### new-paradigms-and-outlook: 30 findings
[0] (confirmed; conf high) Google's Nested Learning / HOPE (NeurIPS 2025) is a proof-of-concept architecture; the blog post claims Nested Learning can mitigate or avoid forgetting but reports no quantitative forgetting results, only language modelling, common-sense reasoning and needle-in-haystack comparisons.
   EV: Blog (7 Nov 2025): Hope "demonstrates lower perplexity and higher accuracy compared to modern recurrent models"; forgetting defined as "where learning new tasks sacrifices proficiency on old tasks". No model sizes or forgetting numbers in the post.
   SRC: Introducing Nested Learning: A new ML paradigm for continual learning (Google Research blog) | Ali Behrouz, Vahab Mirrokni (Google Research) | 2025 | https://research.google/blog/introducing-nested-learning-a-new-ml-paradigm-for-continual-learning/
[1] (confirmed; conf high) The Nested Learning paper itself says catastrophic forgetting is not solved; HOPE is evaluated at 760M and 1.3B parameters trained from scratch, with small margins over Titans, and continual-learning results on Llama backbones are shown only as figures against ICL, EWC and InCA.
   EV: Conclusion: "the undesirable phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting is not 'solved' in general"; work described as "a roadmap rather than a destination". Table 2 averages: Hope 52.28 vs Titans 51.68 (760M, 30B tokens); Hope 58.04 vs Titans 56.82 (1.3B, 100B tokens).
   SRC: Nested Learning: The Illusion of Deep Learning Architectures (arXiv 2512.24695) | Behrouz, Razaviyayn, Zhong, Mirrokni (Google Research) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2512.24695v1
[2] (confirmed; conf medium) Titans (test-time memorisation) is a long-context memory architecture demonstrated on small models; the Google blog does not show persistent cross-session fact learning in a production-scale LLM.
   EV: Abstract: Titans "can effectively scale to larger than 2M context window size". Blog (4 Dec 2025): "outperforms all baselines, including extremely large models like GPT-4, despite having many fewer parameters"; only 360M and 760M sizes appear in the post's figures.
   SRC: Titans: Learning to Memorize at Test Time (arXiv 2501.00663) and Titans + MIRAS blog | Behrouz, Zhong, Razaviyayn, Mirrokni (Google Research) | 2025 | https://research.google/blog/titans-miras-helping-ai-have-long-term-memory/

[4] (confirmed; conf high) MIT's SEAL improves single-passage knowledge incorporation via self-generated LoRA finetuning data, but the authors report forgetting under sequential self-edits and high compute cost; it does not address retention.

[5] (confirmed; conf high) A 2026 study on Qwen3-4B (LoRA and full finetuning) reproduces the founder's sequential-forgetting pattern: after 20 sequential fact writes, earlier-fact retention is 1% with repeated-statement training and 46% with 24 varied restatements, and general capability drops substantially. Replay, retrieval and joint retraining were not tested.
   EV: "only 1%" vs "46%" after twenty sequential writes; later varied examples raised retention by 37.6 points (95% CI 28.9-46.3); GSM8K/MMLU/format mix fell from 81.0% to 62.3%; "Our sequential writing therefore appears to cause broader capability degradation." [6] (confirmed; conf high) In the same study, behavioural forgetting is often lost access rather than erasure, and errors are mostly interference from later writes.
   EV: Failed fact pairs kept a median 69% of their log-probability gain; of 327 incorrect answers, 68.8% gave a later-written fact; authors: "'forgetting' need not mean erasure". Stated limitation: retention of varied examples "may partly reflect smaller weight updates rather than broader data".

[7] (partly_correct; conf high) Joint (multi-task) LoRA training on all tasks is treated as the upper bound in LLM continual-learning benchmarks, and sequential LoRA falls far below it; classic regularisers (EWC, LwF, GEM) often do worse than plain sequential LoRA.
   EV: Standard CL benchmark, Llama3-8B: MTL 83.02 vs Seq-LoRA 62.48 average (best proposed method 76.19). TRACE on Qwen2.5-7B: MTL 64.28 vs Seq-LoRA 51.54, EWC 44.22. "Both methods represent the upper-bound performance in the continual learning setting."
   SRC: SLoRA: Balancing Plasticity and Forgetting in Large Language Models for Continual Learning (ACL 2026) | Yang, Liao, Wang, Wang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University) | 2026 | https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.247.pdf
   CORRECTION: All quoted numbers and the upper-bound sentence are accurate (read from the PDF). The scope of the regulariser claim is off. On the Standard CL benchmark where the 83.02 vs 62.48 figures come from, EWC (70.01) and LwF (67.41) beat Seq-LoRA (62.48), and so does the untrained base model (69.29); GEM is not reported there. Regularisers fall below Seq-LoRA only on TRACE, where the paper says EWC and GEM 'often fall below' it. The paper also lists single-task learning as an oracle, and STL beats MTL on TRACE (66.52 vs 64.28 on Qwen2.5-7B), so joint training is not a strict ceiling. The gap from MTL to the best sequential method on TRACE is about 5 points (64.28 vs 59.20), much smaller than the headline gap.

--- OVERSTATEMENTS
 * 'Karpathy (three to five more papers)' and 'three to five papers away': the transcript says 'three or four or five more' right after 'some major update to how we do algorithms for LLMs', in a passage about reinforcement learning and reflect-and-review. It is not a count of papers and not a statement about continual learning specifically.
 * 'Amodei is the outlier' / 'Amodei alone suggests one to two years': the researcher's own source (Transformer, 22 Jan 2026) quotes Anthropic's Sholto Douglas predicting continual learning 'gets solved in a satisfying way' in 2026, Patel expects labs to release something called continual learning in 2026, and Pachocki says it is what OpenAI is building. Amodei is not alone on the short end.
 * 'What frontier labs actually shipped in 2026 under this banner is memory outside the weights': true for the Anthropic post cited, but Cursor shipped weight updates from production data every five hours with an eval gate (Mar 2026), and iterative post-training of deployed models is tracked as standard lab practice. The claim holds only for the largest labs' branded 'continual learning' features.
 * 'The closest independent replication of the founder's result' and 'The founder's 0/90 and 43/90 results match the published Qwen3-4B picture': the Qwen3-4B study is not a replication. It used a different model, invented counterfactual facts, one fact per write, and no replay. Its 46% comes from varied restatements; if the founder's 43/90 came from replay, the two numbers arise from different mechanisms and their closeness is coincidental. The founder's numbers could not be checked against any source.
 * 'How each log entry is turned into training text matters more than the adapter method': the supporting study states that update size was not controlled, so varied examples 'may partly' retain more because they produce smaller weight changes. The comparison with adapter methods rests on one O-LoRA test in one preprint.
 * 'Step 3 ... is the joint-training upper bound that continual-learning papers measure themselves against': this is shown for task benchmarks (classification, TRACE), not for packing a growing log of facts into one LoRA. In the same paper single-task training beats joint training on TRACE and the best sequential method is within about 5 points of joint training. No cited source tests joint LoRA retraining on an accumulating fact log, and the cited Qwen3-4B study explicitly did not test joint retraining or replay.
 * 'Because the rebuild starts from the base, replay only has to protect general ability during one training run': no cited source tests this. The Qwen3-4B study saw general-benchmark accuracy fall from 81.0% to 62.3% after 20 sequential writes, and the 'pack into LoRA' literature shows single-run fact finetuning also degrades external QA; neither measures a from-base rebuild with replay.
 * 'even a roughly USD 40M effort (Thomson) shipped with a 4-point coding regression found only through dedicated drift tracking': the 39.9 vs 43.9 coding gap is reported in the paper's main test-evaluation table. The paper does not say it was found only through the 10-15% drift-monitoring subset. The USD 40M figure covers reusable research and infrastructure over a longer period, not the model alone.
 * 'TiC-LM and Ibrahim et al. show continual training with replay can approach retraining at about 2.6x less compute': the 2.6x figure is TiC-LM only; Ibrahim et al. say 'a fraction of the compute'. Both are full-weight pretraining at hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens. Applying them to incremental LoRA updates on a 1.7B model is extrapolation. TiC-LM also found replay hurts on fast-changing domains, which the cost implication omits.
 * 'Sparse memory finetuning ... needs memory-layer architecture that Qwen3 does not have': the 2026 re-implementation the researcher cites added key-value memory layers to a stock Qwen-2.5-0.5B-Instruct, so retrofitting is feasible. That re-implementation measured a medical multiple-choice task (MedMCQA), not a fact stream, so '+2.5 vs +4.6 pp' is not a like-for-like test of fact learning.
 * 'Nightly LoRA consolidation reached 80.4% against a 90.1% full-context ceiling' is used to argue retrieval should stay, but that study did not test retrieval at all, covered 10 software-development conversations, used an LLM judge, and did not measure general benchmarks. It does support the design in one way the researcher did not note: its cumulative mode trains a fresh LoRA on facts from all conversations so far.
 * 'Anthropic found memory plus search (61%) better than either alone': the figures are shares of graded criteria on a 25-question set with Opus 5 agents, with no run counts given; the post also reports tasks where the wiki gave no clear gain over search. It is weak quantitative support.
 * Plasticity loss as 'an argument for restarting from a fixed base': this inference is the researcher's. The source proposes continual backpropagation instead and studies small from-scratch networks over thousands of tasks, not pretrained LLMs with adapters.
 * 'Startups founded on continual learning ... have published no model demonstrating it': rests on one secondary roundup. That roundup notes Pathway has public research code and BDH results up to one billion parameters, and Adaption has shipped products, so 'published nothing' is too strong for those two; SSI's focus on continual learning is inferred from Sutskever's interview rather than stated by the company.
 * 'Forecasts and lab products point to memory outside the weights through 2027' and 'forecast median 2033': these come from a single vendor's forecasting tool (FutureSearch) with an 80% interval of June 2028 to January 2044 and a strict resolution rule (top-ten model, weekly updates driven mainly by deployment data, three months sustained). It is one estimate with a very wide range, not a consensus.
 * 'Hassabis and Sutskever say it is unsolved and years away': Hassabis's 'five to ten years' is his AGI timeline, not a continual-learning timeline; Sutskever's '5 to 20' answers a question about a system that learns like a human and then becomes superhuman. Neither gave a date for continual learning as such.
--- MISSING WORK
 + Cursor, 'Real-time RL for Composer' (26 Mar 2026): a shipped loop that updates model weights from production user interactions, with a new checkpoint about every five hours, gated by regression evals (CursorBench). Reported gains are small (+2.28% edits kept, -3.13% dissatisfied follow-ups) and the post documents two reward-hacking failures. — It contradicts the summary's statement that what was shipped in 2026 under this banner is only memory outside the weights. It is a production weight-update loop with a promotion gate, which is the closest real-world analogue to the design's step 4. The post does not address forgetting. [https://cursor.com/en/blog/real-time-rl-for-composer]
 + Titans Revisited: A Lightweight Reimplementation and Critical Analysis of a Test-Time Memory Model (arXiv 2510.09551, Oct 2025). Independent reimplementation, done because Google released no code; finds Titans 'does not always outperform established baselines due to chunking', though the memory module helps over attention-only models. — A negative replication result for one of the headline new-paradigm architectures. It strengthens the 'proof of concept' reading with independent evidence, which the researcher supported only with the authors' own caveats and press quotes. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09551]
 + How Much Knowledge Can You Pack into a LoRA Adapter without Harming LLM? (arXiv 2502.14502, 2025). Llama-3.1-8B with LoRA: best results when training data mixes known and new facts, but external QA benchmark performance still declines, answers collapse toward over-represented entities, and the model becomes overconfident and rarely refuses. — The design's step 3 assumes one LoRA retrained on the whole log can hold a growing set of facts. This paper is direct evidence on that capacity and side-effect question, which none of the cited sources address. It also supports mixing known facts into the rebuild data. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14502]
 + When Does Continual Learning Require Learning (arXiv 2607.07847, Jul 2026; Berkeley, Darrell/Malik group). Eight methods on Qwen3-8B under one protocol (prompt optimisation, SFT, self-distillation finetuning, GRPO, Cartridges, in-place test-time training). No method wins in all regimes; self-distillation keeps old skills but resists updating facts (stable-fact F1 fell from about 0.30 to 0.15); SFT success on agentic chains decays from 60% at one step to 6.7% at ten. — A 2026 head-to-head on a Qwen3 model that is closer to the founder's setup than most cited work, and a largely negative result: weight methods trade off retention against updating changed facts, which bears on how the log handles superseded values. [https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07847v1]
 + Spurious Forgetting in Continual Learning of Language Models (arXiv 2501.13453, ICLR 2025). Argues that performance drops on old tasks mostly reflect lost task alignment, not erased knowledge, and that freezing bottom layers helps. — Peer-reviewed prior evidence for the 'lost access, not erasure' claim, which the researcher rests on a single 2026 preprint whose own limitations section calls that interpretation indirect. It also offers a concrete mitigation (layer freezing) not considered in the design. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13453]
 + Synthetic continued pretraining / EntiGraph (arXiv 2409.07431). Models need to see a fact in 'hundreds to thousands of diverse representations'; generating an entity-graph synthetic corpus from a small document set lets the model answer without the documents, and the gain adds to retrieval when documents are available. — The strongest established evidence for the implication that varied restatements matter and that retrieval and weight learning are complementary. It suggests 24 restatements per fact may be well below what is needed, a point the researcher's sources do not make. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07431]
 + LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less (arXiv 2405.09673). LoRA substantially underperforms full finetuning on target domains but preserves out-of-domain performance better; full finetuning learns perturbations of 10 to 100 times higher rank. — The standard reference for the learn/forget trade-off of the adapter method the design is built on. It bounds how much new knowledge a low-rank rebuild can be expected to absorb. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673]
 + Rethinking Transfer in Continual Learning: A Replay-Based Realisation (arXiv 2607.15587, Jul 2026). Single LoRA trained continually on Qwen2.5-0.5B and 7B: replay-based methods dominate, every rehearsal-free baseline (EWC, LwF, O-LoRA, GainLoRA and others) forgets more; plain experience replay lifts 0.5B accuracy on TRACE-8 from .264 to .451. — 2026 evidence at small-model scale (0.5B) that replay is the component that matters, which is closer to the founder's 1.7B setting than the 7-8B and pretraining-scale results the researcher cites. [https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15587]
 + LessWrong, 'What's Continual Learning, and Why Might We Expect To See It In Advanced LLM Agents?' (12 Jun 2026). States weight updates 'do not happen automatically in frontier systems', attributes this to naive updates degrading capabilities, and lists the 2026 weight-based exceptions (Cursor real-time RL, Prime Intellect, on-policy distillation from user feedback). — A mid-2026 map of which deployed systems update weights from deployment data. It shows the 'memory only' picture has exceptions the researcher's summary leaves out. [https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5mCJzimtNZc9o4e26/what-s-continual-learning-and-why-might-we-expect-to-see-it]
 + AI 2027 Tracker, 'Models shift to continuous/iterative training' (updated 9 Nov 2026). Rates iterative post-training of deployed models as confirmed standard practice at frontier labs (OpenAI GPT-4o updates, DeepSeek V3.1 continued pretraining), while noting daily online learning and continual pretraining of frontier models remain unproven. — Distinguishes periodic retraining of deployed weights, which labs already do, from true continual learning. The researcher's option (c), 'a lab-released weight-update service', and the 13% forecast for periodic retraining should be read against this. [https://ai2027-tracker.com/predictions/continuous-training/]
