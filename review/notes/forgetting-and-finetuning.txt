##### forgetting-and-finetuning: 22 findings
[0] (confirmed; conf high) LoRA at common ranks learns less than full fine-tuning on a new domain but forgets less of the base model's abilities; forgetting still grows with training duration and with rank.
   EV: Llama-2-7B, code continued pretraining at 20B tokens: forgetting score (avg HellaSwag/ARC-C/WinoGrande) 0.635 at r=16, 0.626 at r=64, 0.617 at r=256, vs 0.545 for full fine-tuning. Authors report forgetting worsens with training duration and is controlled by rank; best LoRA learning rates about 10x those of full fine-tuning; attention-only targeting underperforms MLP/all modules.
   SRC: LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less | Biderman et al. (Columbia / Databricks), TMLR 2024 | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2
[1] (confirmed; conf high) When LoRA updates are merged and a new adapter is trained on top, task after task, LoRA degrades faster than full fine-tuning; low ranks, which create the most 'intruder dimensions', degrade most. LoRA forgetting less is not guaranteed.
   EV: Intruder-dimension count correlates with forgetting: Spearman 0.971 (RoBERTa), 0.59 (LLaMA2-7B). Continual experiment on RoBERTa-base over six tasks with merge-and-reinitialise after each: all LoRA ranks degrade more rapidly than full fine-tuning. alpha=2r yields fewer intruder dimensions than alpha=8.
   SRC: LoRA vs Full Fine-tuning: An Illusion of Equivalence | Shuttleworth, Andreas, Torralba, Sharma (MIT), NeurIPS 2025 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21228v3
[2] (confirmed; conf medium) On 100 sequential QA-memorisation tasks with LoRA on Qwen3-4B, naive sequential training retains almost nothing and the best stack of known mitigations still loses about two thirds; general ability collapses too.
   EV: Qwen3-4B-Base, LoRA r=32 alpha 64. Final retention: naive 1.2%; best single mechanism 8.1%; best composite (pseudo-replay from the previous model + function anchor + SI weight anchor + merged LoRA) 34.9%. Replay main effect +9.5 to +19.3 points; SI regularisation only +0.3 to +6.3. General benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MGSM, MMLU-Redux): base 69.4% average, 13.0-13.4% after 100 tasks with merged LoRA, 26.8-28.8% with O-LoRA. No joint/cumulative upper bound is reported.
   SRC: Continual Learning Mechanisms Compose for Long-Horizon Memorization | Zhang, Zhang, Khashabi, Shu (Johns Hopkins), preprint Sep 2026 | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2609.06986
[3] (confirmed; conf medium) In 20 sequential merged LoRA fact-writes on Qwen3-4B, the training-data format decides retention: bare repeated statements retain about 1%, varied examples about 46%; O-LoRA does not help; general ability drops by about 19 points.
   EV: LoRA r=16, alpha 32, lr 2e-4, 24 examples per fact, each write merged before the next. Strict retention of facts 1-19 after 20 writes: 1% (repeated statements) vs 46% (paraphrases, QA, consequences, contrasts); second fact set 2.9% vs 36.8%. O-LoRA 46.7% vs 44.4% without. General benchmark (100 items) 81.0% to 62.3%. 68.8% of wrong answers returned content of a later-written fact. With the fact in the prompt: 91% (lenient). Neither replay nor cumulative retraining from base was tested.
   SRC: Can a Language Model Continually Learn Facts in its Weights? | O'Neill, Kirkby, Liu, Psenka (Base Labs / Baseten), workshop preprint | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11020
[4] (confirmed; conf high) Joint (multi-task) training is the upper bound in LoRA continual instruction tuning; sequential training without rehearsal collapses; rehearsal with data synthesised by the base model nearly closes the gap and beats 10% real-data rehearsal.
   EV: Llama-2-7b, LoRA r=8 on q,v, 5 SuperNI tasks: multi-task 53.05 average ROUGE-L; non-rehearsal 19.03 (BWT -42.39); 1% real rehearsal 49.67 (-4.02); 10% real 50.32 (-3.02); self-synthesized rehearsal 52.16 (-0.79). 10 tasks: multi-task 64.69; non-rehearsal 17.33 (-53.64); SSR 63.23 (-1.56). MMLU on Alpaca-7b: 41.0 base, 34.1 non-rehearsal, 37.1 SSR. The base model, not the latest checkpoint, is used to synthesise because in-context ability degrades after fine-tuning.
   SRC: Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting in Large Language Models with Self-Synthesized Rehearsal | Huang et al., ACL 2024 | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01244v2
[5] (confirmed; conf medium) On a 24-month stream of changing Wikidata facts with 1-3B models, cumulative (full) replay LoRA retains most facts and retention rises with rank, while sequential LoRA and O-LoRA retain far less; retrieval retains nearly everything with no training.
   EV: Llama-3.2-1B, 4,739 changed facts. Full-replay time-averaged retention (memorised form / generalised form): r8 0.846/0.741; r16 0.911/0.797; r32 0.953/0.878; r64 0.976/0.937; r72 0.979/0.947. Stability on unchanged facts falls from 0.739 (r8) to 0.682 (r72). Sequential LoRA 0.657; O-LoRA 0.644; RAG 0.999. Qwen2.5-1.5B: 0.818 (r8), 0.984 (r72). Caveats: paper does not state whether full replay restarts from base or warm-starts; single author; some single-seed runs; multiple-choice scoring.
   SRC: Beyond Endpoint Scores (temporal knowledge updating with LoRA on small LLMs) | Heejin Choi, preprint Sep 2026 | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03900v1
[6] (partly_correct; conf medium) On Llama 3 8B, sequential LoRA with 10% replay of earlier data matches or slightly beats joint LoRA training on preserving old knowledge, and LoRA preserves more than full fine-tuning; TIES-style merging performs poorly.
   EV: 7 TRACE tasks. Old-knowledge averages: base 0.6611; LoRA + replay 0.6556; sequential LoRA 0.6541; joint LoRA 0.6524; full FT + replay 0.6067. Without replay, LoRA backward transfer on a science task was -41% (0.8895 to 0.531 after a maths task). TIES and TIES-DARE reported as not performing well; O-LoRA with small weight (lambda <= 0.5) described as a middle ground.
   SRC: Collaboratively adding new knowledge to an LLM | Zaman, Choshen, Srivastava et al. (preprint) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14753
   CORRECTION: The old-knowledge averages are as quoted (Table 5), but 'Old Knowledge' here means general benchmarks of pre-training knowledge (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag etc.), not retention of earlier tasks. Plain sequential LoRA with no replay scores 0.6541, so replay adds nothing measurable on this metric and the spread across the LoRA variants is about 0.003. On the fine-tuned tasks (AVnew, Table 6) replay beats joint training outright: FFT-Rep 0.621, LoRA-Rep 0.613, Joint-FFT 0.603, sequential LoRA 0.595, sequential FFT 0.589, Joint-LoRA 0.585 (lowest); the paper calls joint training 'slightly inferior'. The -41% backward transfer on science is measured from 0.8975 (score right after its own training) to 0.531; 0.8895 is the score after round 6, before the maths task. The LoRA rank is not stated, and O-LoRA/TIES results are in figures only.
[7] (partly_correct; conf high) For factoid memorisation specifically, forgetting is worst when the later training stage is also factoids; replaying 10% of old factoids is not enough, EWC-style regularisation is weak, and mixing in generic unrelated data works much better.
   EV: Llama-3-8B / Mistral-7B, 2,000 stage-1 factoids. Stage-1 retention after a factoid second stage: 13.5% (KVR), 47.0% (PopQA), 39.8% (TriviaQA). 10% replay reaches only 41.6% in the worst case. REMIX (generic/random data mixed at about 1:2) lifts 13.5% to 53.2%; average on KVR: REMIX 67.4% vs weight regularisation 27.3% vs behaviour regularisation 17.5%.
   SRC: Continual Memorization of Factoids in Language Models (REMIX) | Chen, Geng, Bhaskar, Friedman, Chen (Princeton) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07175v2
   CORRECTION: 41.6% is not the worst case: it is the average over the three factoid stage-2 tasks for KVR at 10% replay (LAMA 13.2, EntityQA 33.3, WebQA 78.2; Table 7). The worst case with 10% replay is 13.2%, essentially no better than no replay. The 67.4 / 27.3 / 17.5 comparison comes from Table 4, where the no-mixing baseline is 17.8% (the 13.5 to 53.2 figure is from Table 2, a different average). REMIX mixes data at 1:2 in both stages (random word sequences in stage 1, Knowledge Pile in stage 2), and the paper does not say whether training was full fine-tuning or LoRA. Other numbers are as quoted.
[8] (confirmed; conf high) In large-scale time-continual pretraining, replay nearly eliminates backward forgetting and matches from-scratch 'Oracle' retraining on all data at lower compute, while EWC and LwF help little; a small persistent gap to full retraining remains and replay hurts fast-changing domains.
   EV: 114 monthly Common Crawl dumps. Backward regret: no replay 0.082; EWC 0.055; LwF 0.082; replay ratio 1/2 0.007; replay 1/t 0.001; Oracle retraining -0.003. Replay matches Oracle with 2.6x less compute. Static downstream score: Oracle 50.6 vs replay 50.1. Replay helps stable domains (NumPy) and hurts fast-changing ones (PyTorch).
   SRC: TiC-LM: A Web-Scale Benchmark for Time-Continual LLM Pretraining | Li et al. (University of Washington / Apple), ACL 2025 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02107v3
[9] (confirmed; conf medium) Samples generated by the model itself are effective replay data for protecting prior abilities, and a KL (distillation) loss on replay data forgets least; plain next-token training on substitute external data can increase forgetting.
   EV: On Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, KL regularisation on substitute or self-generated data almost completely removes forgetting; next-token loss on replay is slightly behind KL; replay batch 4x smaller than the downstream batch by default; replay permits higher fine-tuning learning rates; forgetting persists when the model has little spare capacity. In their small (205M) setting LoRA learned less and forgot more than full fine-tuning. Mostly single-new-task experiments.
   SRC: Forgetting in Language Models: Capacity, Optimization, and Self-Generated Replay | Marek, Cho, Qiu, Chunara, Izmailov, Wilson (NYU), preprint May 2026 | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26097v1
[10] (confirmed; conf medium) Mixing task data 1:1 with dialogues generated by the frozen base model preserves general ability far better than task-only LoRA and better than mixing in external instruction data.
   EV: LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, LoRA r=8 alpha 16, lr 2e-4, 3 epochs. GSM8K: base 75.8; task-only LoRA 65.6; task-only full FT 51.5; task + Alpaca 55.3; task + UltraChat 70.1; self-dialogue mix 74.2 (LoRA), 68.2 (full FT). Ratio sweep 0.25 to 1.75, with 1:1 best. Mitigated forgetting in 50 of 50 settings. Authors note weaker base models may produce repetitive, low-information self-dialogues.
   SRC: Talking to Yourself: self-augmented supervised fine-tuning (Sa-sft) | Sun et al. (Zhejiang University), preprint | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20162
[11] (partly_correct; conf high) Adapter capacity is not the limit for hundreds of facts, but each batch of new facts costs general knowledge and calibration: a rank-1 LoRA on an 8B model learns 500 unknown facts at about 100% reliability while MMLU and refusal behaviour degrade, more so with paraphrase augmentation and more facts.
   EV: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, LoRA rank 1, alpha 2, MLP projections, 10 epochs, lr 1e-3. Reliability 1.0 for 1/10/50 facts, 0.97-1.0 at 500, 0.92-0.98 at 3,000. Mixing in already-known facts reduces harmful shifts but does not remove benchmark losses.

[13] (partly_correct; conf medium) LoRA stores roughly 2 to 3.4 bits per trainable parameter, slightly less than full fine-tuning, so even small adapters have megabytes of raw capacity; LoRA only falls behind full fine-tuning when the dataset exceeds that capacity.
   EV: Bits per trainable parameter: rank 1 2.39; rank 3 2.99; rank 16 3.40; full fine-tuning 3.95. Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B at rank 1: about 2.0-2.5. A rank-16 adapter on a 7B model (about 40M parameters) at 2 bits is about 10 MB. Measured on random sequences, not facts; no forgetting measured. The Thinking Machines study independently cites about 2 bits per parameter, a 10x LoRA learning-rate rule, and that attention-only rank 256 underperforms MLP-only rank 128.
   SRC: How Much Can a LoRA Adapter Memorize? Measuring Adapter Capacity in Bits (with LoRA Without Regret, https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/) | Tan, Du, Feng (preprint); Schulman et al., Thinking Machines Lab 2025 | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21351
   CORRECTION: The 2.39 / 2.99 / 3.40 / 3.95 bits-per-parameter figures were measured on a 2.2M-parameter toy transformer (adapters of 12K-197K parameters) memorising uniformly random token sequences, not on a real LLM. The paper does not test whether LoRA falls behind full fine-tuning only beyond capacity; that statement is from the Thinking Machines post ('For datasets that exceed LoRA capacity, LoRA underperforms FullFT'), which itself takes the 2-bits figure from prior work on synthetic data rather than measuring it. The 10x learning-rate rule and the attention-only r256 vs MLP-only r128 result are as quoted.

--- OVERSTATEMENTS
 * 'Joint/cumulative training is the upper bound in every study that reports one' is contradicted by the researcher's own source [6]: in Collaboratively adding new knowledge, Joint-LoRA is the lowest LoRA entry on the trained tasks (AVnew 0.585 vs LoRA-Rep 0.613 and FFT-Rep 0.621) and also below LoRA-Rep on old knowledge; the paper calls joint training 'slightly inferior'. Only SSR, Soup-to-go and TiC-LM show joint/oracle on top, and TiC-LM's margin is 0.5 points.
 * The 0.98 cumulative-replay figure [5] cannot support 'retrain from base': the paper does not say whether the adapter is re-initialised each month, measures no general ability at all (no MMLU or similar), scores by multiple choice against same-relation distractors, is single-author, and shows stability on unchanged facts falling as rank rises (0.739 to 0.682).
 * 'Retrieval beats weights for fresh facts everywhere it was compared' is too strong even within the cited sources: in [17] task-specific Active Reading (66.25) exceeds the gold-context baseline (65.85). Outside them, prompt distillation is level with RAG on Llama-3-8B, and RING is level with reranked RAG on Qwen3-14B.
 * 'A trained adapter plus retrieved context beats either alone (62.60 vs 60.35 vs 56.22)' rests on EntiGraph only (full continued pretraining, a 2.25-point gain). The researcher's other sources show the opposite: in [18] fine-tuned + RAG is below base + RAG for both models (Mistral 0.810/0.830 vs 0.875; Llama2 0.326/0.520 vs 0.585), and in [14] on QuALITY single LoRA + ICL (64.12) is below ICL alone (70.20) and multi-LoRA + RAG (60.34) is below RAG alone (63.79). The fine-tuned adapter can make retrieval worse, so 'evaluate with and without retrieval' is right but the expected direction is not established.
 * The KL-replay recommendation leans on one experiment that is not LoRA and not sequential: Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, full fine-tuning, a single Verilog task, forgetting measured on MMLU/CommonsenseQA/ARC. In the same paper's only LoRA experiment (205M model) LoRA learned less and forgot more. In [3], distillation-style writes retained less (13-31%) than plain varied examples (46%), which is a different use of KL but shows it is not uniformly better.
 * 'Skip O-LoRA' is stated more firmly than the evidence allows. For fact retention it gave no gain ([3], [5]), but in [2] O-LoRA kept roughly twice the general ability of merged LoRA after 100 tasks (26.8-28.8% vs 13.0-13.4%), and in the SLAO paper it adds 1.2 to 4.8 points. Its effect on general ability, which is one of the founder's two problems, is positive in the researcher's own source.
 * 'EWC-style 27.3 vs 67.4' and '10% replay is not enough' come from REMIX, a two-stage setup with unstated tuning method; the '41.6% worst case' is actually a three-task average (true worst case 13.2%). The replay-ratio guidance ('10% is the floor', 'start at 1:1', 'up to 2:1') is stitched from papers with different meanings of replay: old-task examples (SSR, [6]), generic pretraining text (EntiGraph, Active Reading), self-dialogues for task tuning (Sa-sft), and random word sequences (REMIX). None tested these ratios for fact logs on a 1-4B LoRA.
 * 'Capacity is not the constraint' is over-read. The bits-per-parameter numbers [13] are from a 2.2M-parameter toy model on random tokens; the megabyte figure is an extrapolation; and the rank-1 / 500-facts result [11] is on an 8B model with reliability measured on the trained question form. In [12] rank 8 on Qwen3-4B never got past about 79% paraphrase accuracy, and [5] shows retention rising steeply with rank (0.846 at r8 to 0.979 at r72 on 1B), so at 1-4B low rank does look binding for usable recall.
 * 'Start at r=8-16' from [12] carries an unflagged confound: that study used 100 epochs at lr 2e-4 with no replay and alpha apparently scaling with rank, and the authors do not rule out the rank-64 collapse being an effective-learning-rate effect. The rank-vs-damage curve may look very different with replay and early stopping.
 * 'Early stopping tied to the gate' is supported by one paper on PaLM 2-S QA fine-tuning; Kalajdzievski (2024) concludes forgetting under LoRA 'cannot be avoided through early stopping or by varying the number of parameters'. The two measure different things (hallucination on known facts vs general forgetting), so early stopping should be presented as partial help, not a control.
 * 'About 20-25 varied examples per fact is the scale used in the closest study' is one data point ([3], 24 examples, 20 facts in sequence, about 46% retention and a 19-point general-ability drop measured on a 100-item benchmark). The '46% vs 1%' gain is for merged sequential writes, not for the retrain-from-base design; no source shows how much example variety matters once all facts are trained jointly.
 * 'Do not put the fact in the prompt of the training examples' generalises contextual shadowing from one paper ([19]); prompt distillation (Kujanpaa et al.) deliberately uses the in-context model as teacher and gets the best closed-book numbers found, so context is harmful as student input but useful for generating targets.
 * 'The literature matches the founder's results closely' and 'sits where the literature predicts' are not testable claims: the cited retention numbers span 1% to 98% across setups that differ in model, rank, merge policy, example format and scoring, so almost any result would 'match'.
 * The summary says evidence on 1-4B models is 'now reasonable', but the load-bearing 2026 sources at that scale are four preprints, at least one single-author with single-seed sweep points ([5]), and the Qwen3-1.7B evidence is thin: a standard-LoRA control with no matched baseline in [12] (OOD 57.0 / 52.0 / 40.2 at r8/16/32) and a figure-only scaling curve in [14].
 * The TiC-LM 'supersede stale facts' implication is an extrapolation: TiC-LM shows replay of old web crawls hurting a fast-changing domain (PyTorch) in from-scratch-scale pretraining; it does not test fact-level supersede markers. The recommendation is sensible but not evidenced by that paper.
--- MISSING WORK
 + Self-Adapting Language Models (SEAL), Zweiger et al., MIT, NeurIPS 2025. The model writes its own training data ('self-edits') and applies LoRA updates; on Qwen2.5-7B no-context SQuAD goes 33.5% (passage only) to 47.0%, vs 46.3% with GPT-4.1 synthetic data. — It is the closest published system to the founder's design (self-generated study data plus LoRA writes), and its limitations section reports that performance on earlier passages declines steadily as sequential self-edits accumulate, with no fix offered. [https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2]
 + Continual Learning via Sparse Memory Finetuning, Lin et al., FAIR at Meta, Oct 2025. On a 1.3B model learning a stream of TriviaQA facts, NaturalQuestions F1 drops 89% with full fine-tuning and 71% with LoRA (best of a rank/alpha/lr sweep) but 11% when only the most-activated memory-layer slots are updated. — A negative result for LoRA as a fact store at exactly the founder's model scale, and an alternative (sparse updates, no replay) that the summary does not consider. It did not test replay, so it does not settle LoRA-plus-replay vs sparse memory. [https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15103v1]
 + On Warm-Starting Neural Network Training, Ash and Adams, NeurIPS 2020. Warm-starting from a previous solution generalises worse than fresh initialisation at similar training loss. — It is the standard evidence for the from-base vs warm-start question the researcher says no paper addresses. It supports step 3 in direction, but it is on vision/small networks trained from random init, not LoRA on a pretrained LLM, so it remains indirect. [https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.08475]
 + Scaling Laws for Forgetting When Fine-Tuning Large Language Models, Kalajdzievski, Jan 2024. With LoRA on Llama 2 7B chat, forgetting follows a shifted power law in parameters tuned and update steps, and is inversely linear in fine-tuning loss. — It concludes forgetting cannot be avoided through early stopping or by varying the number of parameters tuned, which cuts against the implications that low rank and gate-tied early stopping will control collateral damage without replay. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05605]
 + Efficient Knowledge Injection in LLMs via Self-Distillation (prompt distillation), Kujanpaa et al., Dec 2024, revised Aug 2025. A LoRA student (rank 512-1024, on Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3-8B, Qwen2.5-14B) is distilled from the same model reading the document in context. — A counter-example to 'retrieval beats weights everywhere': closed-book Llama-3-8B reaches 86.1 / 94.4 / 93.6 / 79.5 vs RAG 86.3 / 95.6 / 96.3 / 78.6 on Squadshifts, far above plain fine-tuning (75.9 / 89.5 / 87.5 / 69.8). It still trails RAG on Qwen2.5-14B and HotpotQA (73.7 vs 79.5), uses very high rank, and tests no sequential updates. [https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14964v2]
 + RING: Retrieval-Internalized Generation for Continual Large-Scale Knowledge Injection, Xu et al., Aug 2026 (Qwen3-8B/14B, News-2025 benchmark). — A 2026 result on sequential vs mixed injection at 20k-100k documents: sequential full fine-tuning falls from about 27% to about 12%; LoRA and KBLAM also degrade; with mixed training plus replay baselines plateau around 18-25%. On Qwen3-8B a reranked RAG pipeline still beats the parametric method (39.51 vs 35.08), on 14B it is roughly level. Shows joint-plus-replay still loses ground as the corpus grows. [https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01630]
 + How new data permeates LLM knowledge and how to dilute it, Sun et al., Google DeepMind, Apr 2025 (ICLR 2025). Learning a new fact 'primes' the model to apply it in unrelated contexts; stepping-stone text augmentation and ignore-k update pruning cut this by 50-95%. — Directly about collateral damage from few-example fact learning, a failure mode the proposed evaluation gate does not test (new facts leaking into unrelated answers), and it gives two mitigations that are not replay. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09522]
 + Mask the Target: A Plug-and-Play Regularizer Against LoRA Forgetting, Xu et al., May 2026. A replay-free KL term over the non-target vocabulary against the base model's distribution. — A 2026 LoRA-specific alternative to the replay-with-KL recommendation that needs no replay data. Only the abstract was checked and it gives no numbers, so treat as a lead, not a result. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29498]
 + Doc-to-LoRA: Learning to Instantly Internalize Contexts, Charakorn et al., Sakana AI, ICML 2026. A hypernetwork emits a LoRA adapter for a document in one forward pass (experiments on Gemma-2-2b-it). — A different route to the 'write a fact into weights' step on a 2B model that removes per-fact gradient training; relevant as an alternative to the fast path or to stacking adapters. Abstract-level check only; it does not address accumulation across many documents. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902]
 + Spurious Forgetting in Continual Learning of Language Models, Zheng et al., ICLR 2025. Much apparent forgetting is lost task alignment in the first optimisation steps rather than erased knowledge; freezing bottom layers helps. — Supports the O'Neill et al. reading that forgotten facts may still be present, and suggests a cheap mitigation (layer freezing) the summary does not mention. Search snippets indicate it still leaves a gap to replay; only the abstract was opened. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13453]
