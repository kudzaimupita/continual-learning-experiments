--- OVERSTATEMENTS
 * PersonaMem-v2 is used three times (summary, finding 25, implication a) as evidence for a weight-update path. The fine-tuned Qwen3-4B was trained on other personas and still read the history in context, so it learned a skill, not user facts; the best result (55%) was a 2k-token text memory. The paper tested long-context input, not retrieval, so it is also not evidence of a 'retrieval limit'. If anything it supports training the adapter on how to use the log, not on the log's contents.
 * The list of cases where 'a weight-update slow path is justified' rests entirely on evidence that retrieval or long context is weak there. None of the cited sources tests a weight path on aggregation, multi-hop conflict resolution, long logs or latency. The direct evidence found points the other way: closed-book LoRA scored far below RAG and ICL (arXiv 2603.01097), and weight-edited models fail on multi-hop questions where external memory does better (MQuAKE).
 * Implication (d) cites LongMemEval drops and NoLiMa as reasons for a weight path once the log outgrows the model's reading ability. Both papers compare long context with focused or oracle context, so the remedy they support is retrieval. Neither tested a model near 1.7B (the smallest in LongMemEval is Phi-3.5 Mini), and in LongMemEval the 70B model dropped more than the 8B, so 'small models lose more' is not shown.
 * Implication (e) cites 17.1 s vs 1.44 s p95 as a latency case for weights. That figure is Mem0's retrieval against a 26k-token full context; it shows retrieval solving the latency problem and says nothing about adapters.
 * 'Steps 1 and 2 match what Anthropic and Google actually do; keep the log verbatim' goes beyond the sources. Anthropic's memory tool has Claude create, rewrite, rename and delete files, and Claude's consumer memory is a synthesised summary; both are model-derived, mutable memory, not an append-only verbatim log. Google's post gives no mechanism at all.
 * 'Derived memories add cost and accumulate errors, so keep the log verbatim' is stated as settled, but the researcher's own finding 34 (Mastra's compressed observation log at 84.23% vs 80.05% for its RAG on gpt-4o, vendor self-report) and A-Mem matching one-round RAG in Memory Tax are counter-examples. HaluMem's update-stage hallucination rates were under 2%; the dominant failure it reports is omission, and MemOS reached 74-82% extraction recall. The supported statement is narrower: derived memory has not been shown to beat raw-history retrieval reliably and costs more to write.
 * The summary calls the Letta result 'a plain file-plus-grep agent'. The agent also had embedding-based semantic search over the files, so it is not a grep-only baseline.
 * 'Simple BM25 or verbatim-embedding RAG and long-context baselines match or beat specialised memory systems' mixes backbones in MemoryAgentBench (long-context Claude 3.7 and GPT-4o against memory agents on GPT-4o-mini with coarse 4096-token chunks). MemDelta's Mem0 comparison covers 88 instances from 2 of 6 question types, is a single-author preprint, and its Claude full-context figure of 14% was mostly refusals. EvoMemBench reports that memory helps on hard and execution-oriented tasks. 'At far lower write cost' comes from MemDelta alone.
 * The claim that general-knowledge loss will 'grow with log size' leans on Pletenev et al., whose MMLU numbers are not monotonic: 0.615 at 10 facts, about 0.66 at 50 and 100 facts, 0.649 at 500, 0.579 at 3,000 without augmentation. The 0.441 figure is one cell (3,000 facts with 10 paraphrases each). The 10-fact dip looks like run-to-run variance, and the extrapolation from an 8B model to 1.7B is untested.
 * 'Published head-to-heads say these usually win on single-hop fact recall (Ovadia 0.875 vs 0.588)' generalises from multiple-choice tests of unsupervised full fine-tuning on 7B models. The design uses LoRA on QA-style data on a 1.7B model at tens to hundreds of facts, a regime none of the cited head-to-heads covers; Pletenev et al. show 500 facts learned at 100% reliability by LoRA at that small scale.
 * ClashEval's 'over 60%' is the rate at which models adopt incorrect retrieved content over a correct prior, measured on six large models including GPT-4o with deliberately perturbed documents. Applying it as a general override rate for a 1.7B model with a fine-tuned adapter is an extrapolation; adapter-trained facts may be held with different confidence than pretraining priors.
 * The combination evidence in the implications ('about +2 in EntiGraph, cumulative in Balaguer, best-overall in PT-RAG') is presented as mixed but leaves out the personalisation head-to-head where PEFT added about one point on top of RAG, and the RAFT result that the outcome depends on whether retrieval was in the training prompt.
 * Several vendor items are weaker than their placement suggests: Anthropic's 39% / 29% / 84% figures are from an internal evaluation with no dataset or method disclosed; Mastra's scores are self-reported with no replication; the ChatGPT memory description could not be opened and newer FAQ snippets describe a different 'improved memory'. The LoCoMo audit is an anonymous GitHub repository (created February 2026, 23 stars), not a paper.
--- MISSING WORK
 + Memory Tax: Dissecting the Net Value of Agent Memory (He et al., arXiv 2610.34073, Oct 2026). Tests 11 memory systems (A-Mem, SimpleMem, MemOS, MemoryOS, LightMem, OpenViking, HippoRAG2, PlugMem, MAGMA, Mem-T, Memory-R2) against naive RAG and full context on LongMemEval-s, PersonaMem-v2 and MINT-Eval with three backbones including Qwen3.5-4B. Two-round RAG scored 72.2 / 81.4 / 90.0 on LongMemEval-s; most memory systems trailed it, and full context (58.6 / 68.4 / 84.0) was below RAG on all three backbones. Mem0, Zep and Letta are not among the tested systems. — The most recent and broadest controlled negative result for derived memory versus raw-history retrieval, and it includes a 4B backbone close to the design's model size. It also shows full context losing to RAG on small models, which contradicts treating 'whole log in prompt' as the stronger baseline, and it reports that adding raw-history retrieval alongside memory reduces the loss. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34073]
 + Understanding LoRA as Knowledge Memory: An Empirical Analysis (Back et al., arXiv 2603.01097, ICML 2026). Closed-book single LoRA scored well below RAG and ICL (Llama-3.1-8B on QuALITY: ICL 70.20, RAG 63.79, LoRA 42.42). LoRA + RAG beat RAG on NarrativeQA but not consistently on QuALITY. Synthetic QA data beat raw text. Capacity is finite per rank. Multi-LoRA routing and merging degrade. Scaling the base model from 1.7B to 8B gave minimal gains. Includes Qwen3-1.7B. — A direct 2026 test of the exact slow path under consideration (LoRA as fact memory, including the same 1.7B Qwen3 family), with the combined LoRA + retrieval condition the researcher says the gate must use. It concludes LoRA is a complement, not a standalone memory. [https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01097v2]
 + Comparing Retrieval-Augmentation and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for Privacy-Preserving Personalization of LLMs (Salemi and Zamani, arXiv 2409.09510). On LaMP personalisation tasks RAG gave a 14.92% average improvement over the non-personalised model, per-user PEFT gave 1.07%, and combining them gave 15.98%. — A head-to-head on per-user data, which is closer to the design's use case than Ovadia's current-events test. It shows the per-user adapter adding about one point on top of retrieval, which bears directly on the promotion rule. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09510]
 + MQuAKE: Assessing Knowledge Editing in Language Models via Multi-Hop Questions (Zhong et al., arXiv 2305.14795). Weight-edited models recall the edited fact but 'fail catastrophically' on multi-hop questions that depend on it; an external-memory method (MeLLo) outperforms the weight editors by a wide margin. — A negative result against implication (c). The researcher lists multi-hop and conflict resolution as a place where a weight path is justified, but the available evidence says weight updates are worse than external memory at propagating changed facts across hops. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14795]
 + RAFT: Adapting Language Model to Domain Specific RAG (Zhang et al., arXiv 2403.10131). Fine-tuning with retrieved documents and distractors in the training prompt, so the model learns to ignore unhelpful passages and cite the right one, improves in-domain RAG on PubMed, HotpotQA and Gorilla. — Explains why naive fine-tune + RAG fell below RAG alone in Ovadia and offers the known remedy: train the adapter with retrieval in the loop. This is a third option the summary omits, where weights improve the use of retrieved text and do not store the facts. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10131]
 + Cartridges: Lightweight and general-purpose long context representations via self-study (Eyuboglu et al., arXiv 2506.06266). A trained KV-cache per corpus, built from synthetic self-generated conversations with a context-distillation objective, matches in-context performance with 38.6x less memory and 26.4x higher throughput; plain next-token training on the corpus was not competitive. — A middle path between retrieval and LoRA that targets exactly the latency and context-budget case the researcher uses to justify a weight path, with distillation from the in-context teacher as the training target. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266]
 + Doc-to-LoRA: Learning to Instantly Internalize Contexts (Charakorn et al., Sakana AI, arXiv 2602.15902, listed at ICML 2026). A hypernetwork produces a LoRA from a document in one forward pass as approximate context distillation; near-perfect on needle tasks beyond 4x the native window and better than per-document distillation under limited compute. — A 2026 alternative to retraining the adapter from the whole log on every cycle; relevant to the cost of the slow path and reported on a 2B-class base model. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902]
 + In Defense of RAG in the Era of Long-Context Language Models (Yu et al., arXiv 2409.01666). Order-preserving RAG reaches higher answer quality than full long-context input with far fewer tokens; quality follows an inverted U as more chunks are added. — A counter-result to finding 32 (long context beats RAG on average). Together with MemDelta and Memory Tax it shows the full-context versus RAG ranking is not settled and depends on the model. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01666]
 + Mem0, 'State of AI Agent Memory 2026' (updated Oct 2026): Mem0 now self-reports 92.5 on LoCoMo and 94.4 on LongMemEval for its April 2026 algorithm, with no independent replication cited. — The vendor numbers in the summary are 2025 vintage. The current claim sits just under the 93.57% ceiling that the LoCoMo audit computed, which strengthens the point that LoCoMo scores at this level say little, and shows the 66.88 figure no longer describes the product. [https://mem0.ai/blog/locomo-benchmark]
