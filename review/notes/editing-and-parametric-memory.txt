##### editing-and-parametric-memory: 25 findings
[0] (confirmed; conf high) Sequential editing with ROME and MEMIT shows gradual forgetting followed by abrupt catastrophic forgetting; ROME can hit it after as few as 100 edits, MEMIT after roughly 1,400 (GPT-J) to 4,000 (GPT2-XL). A single 'disabling edit' can leave the model unable to accept further edits or do downstream tasks.
   EV: ROME collapse point: as early as 100 edits, as late as 1,000. MEMIT: before 1,500 edits for GPT-J (about 1,400 in main text), around 4,000 for GPT2-XL. Disabling edits observed for ROME, not MEMIT.
   SRC: Model Editing at Scale leads to Gradual and Catastrophic Forgetting | Gupta, Rao, Anumanchipalli (UC Berkeley) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2401.07453v4
[1] (confirmed; conf medium) Even a single edit can trigger model collapse in locate-then-edit methods; the authors propose perplexity as a cheap proxy to detect it.
   EV: Abstract reports that one edit can cause collapse seen as degraded performance across benchmark tasks; perplexity proposed as a surrogate metric. Findings of ACL 2024.
   SRC: The Butterfly Effect of Model Editing: Few Edits Can Trigger Large Language Models Collapse | Yang, Sun, Ma, Liu, Yin, Cheng | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09656
[2] (confirmed; conf medium) Across editing methods, general abilities are preserved only within a few dozen edits; with more edits the model's knowledge structure is damaged and safety behaviour weakens. Larger and instruction-tuned models are more robust (bad news for a 1.7B model).
   EV: Abstract: general abilities hold within a few dozen edits; decline is unavoidable at larger counts; safety significantly weakened even for safety-aligned models; larger models resist editing better. NeurIPS 2024.
   SRC: Should We Really Edit Language Models? On the Evaluation of Edited Language Models | Li, Liu, Tang, Dong, Li, Pan, Chu | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18785
   CORRECTION: Abstract matches. The "bad news for a 1.7B model" remark is the researcher's inference, not something the paper tests.
[3] (partly_correct; conf high) Under realistic (autoregressive, no teacher forcing) evaluation, single-edit success is 38.5% rather than the 96.8% previously reported, and after 1,000 sequential edits every specialised editor is near zero while plain fine-tuning (FT-M) is the best method.
   EV: "current approaches fail drastically with only 1000 edits". Table 9 reliability after 1,000 edits (Llama-2-7b / Mistral-7b / Llama-3-8b): ROME 0.001/0.001/0.001; MEMIT 0.002/0.002/0.000; GRACE 0.015/0.018/0.022; WISE 0.195/0.060/0.072; FT-M 0.531/0.454/0.229. Average excluding FT-M 9.3%; FT-M 40.5%.
   SRC: The Mirage of Model Editing: Revisiting Evaluation in the Wild | Yang, Sun, Tan, Ma, Cao, Yin, Shen, Cheng (ICT CAS), ACL 2025 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11177v5
   CORRECTION: 38.5% vs 96.8% is in the abstract (ACL 2025 main) and the Table 9 values are as quoted. Two points are off. (1) The paper's text says "all methods except FT-M ... only 9.3% average reliability", but 9.3% is the mean of the table's Average row, which includes FT-M; the non-FT-M editors average roughly 3%. (2) "Every specialised editor is near zero" is too strong for WISE, which scores 0.195 on Llama-2-7b (0.060 and 0.072 on the other two). 38.5% is the six-editor average on Llama-2-7b-chat on QAEdit only.
[4] (confirmed; conf high) On a real 502K-edit lifelong benchmark, ROME/MEMIT collapse within about 250 updates, WISE decays to pre-update accuracy, RAG is best by a wide margin, and continual LoRA fine-tuning degrades as updates accumulate unless adapters are merged with interpolation.
   EV: WikiBigEdit: 502,382 QA pairs over 8 timesteps. Average update accuracy: RAG 95.06-99.25%; LoRA-FT 29.21-63.07% (Mistral locality falls to 21.43 from 47.97); LoRA-Merge 60.13-67.36%; pre-update 40.6-49.9%. MEMIT collapses after about 250 sequential edits; WISE converges to pre-update performance within the first timestep. RAG roughly doubles forward-pass time; multi-hop gains from RAG are marginal (only 10% of multi-hop samples retrieve both facts).
   SRC: WikiBigEdit: Understanding the Limits of Lifelong Knowledge Editing in LLMs | Thede, Roth, Bethge, Akata, Hartvigsen, ICML 2025 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2
   CORRECTION: Table 5 ranges and the Mistral locality figures match. Two scope notes: the ~250-edit collapse is stated for MEMIT (ROME/R-ROME are described as collapsing "within the first few hundred updates"), and WISE was only followed over the first timestep / first ~10K updates, not the full 502K. RAG also drops to 78.64% on rephrased and 66.16% on persona questions.
[5] (confirmed; conf high) AlphaEdit (null-space projection, ICLR 2025 oral) was originally shown only at 2,000 edited facts (20 batches of 100), with general capability checked up to 3,000.
   EV: LLaMA3-8B CounterFact after 2,000 edits: AlphaEdit efficacy 98.90 / generalization 94.22 / specificity 67.88 versus MEMIT 65.65 / 64.65 / 51.56. General capability reported as maintained after 3,000 samples.
   SRC: AlphaEdit: Null-Space Constrained Knowledge Editing for Language Models | Fang, Jiang, Wang, Ma, Jie, Wang, He, Chua | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02355v4
   CORRECTION: 2,000 edits in batches of 100, the Table 1 numbers and the 3,000-sample capability statement all match. The ICLR 2025 oral status is not stated on the arXiv page and I did not check it separately.
[6] (confirmed; conf high) An independent 2026 reproduction found AlphaEdit's protection is bounded: by 5,000 sequential edits efficacy falls to about chance on all four models tested, and downstream ability and refusal behaviour collapse; the 3B model broke by 2,000 edits.
   EV: CounterFact efficacy at 2k vs 5k edits: Llama3.2-3B 97.45 -> 48.24; Llama3-8B 99.25 -> 49.02; GPT2-XL 99.50 -> 49.22; GPT-J 99.80 -> 48.28. BoolQ F1: Llama3-8B 0.79 (0 edits) -> 0.08 (9k-10k); Llama3.2-3B 0.58 -> 0.07 at 2k. XSTest drops to 0.33 for all models by 7k. Non-monotonic recovery on GPT-J at 10k (97.83).
   SRC: Reproducibility Study of "AlphaEdit: Null-Space Constrained Knowledge Editing for Language Models" | Suresh, Hariharan | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26783v2
   CORRECTION: All quoted numbers match Tables 5 and 7. Caveats the claim underplays: this is an arXiv preprint by two independent authors with no venue; all four models land at an almost identical 48-49% at 5k; and the authors themselves say GPT-J's rebound to 97.83 at 10k may be "an evaluation artifact". Llama3-8B's XSTest is still 0.84 at 5k and only drops to 0.33 at 6k.
[7] (confirmed; conf medium) A January 2026 paper attributes locate-then-edit collapse to exponential growth of the edited weight norm and reports collapse points of about 400 edits for MEMIT and about 4k for AlphaEdit in one-edit-per-step streams; its norm-anchoring fix pushes these to about 1.5k and beyond 21k (self-reported, code not yet released).
   EV: Full CounterFact stream of 20,877 atomic edits on LLaMA3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, GPT-J. MEMIT collapse about 400 -> about 1.5k (3.75x); AlphaEdit about 4k -> >21k (>5.25x). Baseline GLUE-style scores fall to near zero within the first few thousand edits (typically <=4k). Average editing success 51.9 -> 89.3.
   SRC: Toward Ultra-Long-Horizon Sequential Model Editing | Liu, Zhu, Miao, Fujisawa (Institute of Science Tokyo) | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02543v1
[8] (confirmed; conf medium) GRACE (codebook of key-value adaptors, base weights untouched) retains edits and locality over thousands of edits but does not generalise to rephrasings, making it effectively an exact-match cache.
   EV: Original claim: first method enabling thousands of sequential edits (NeurIPS 2023). Independent tables: generalization 0.08 at T=1000 and 0.03 at T=3000 on LLaMA-2-7B ZsRE (WISE paper); 0.28 at 15,000 edits (MEMOIR paper); wild reliability 0.012-0.022 (Mirage paper).
   SRC: Aging with GRACE: Lifelong Model Editing with Discrete Key-Value Adaptors | Hartvigsen, Sankaranarayanan, Palangi, Kim, Ghassemi | 2023 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11031
   CORRECTION: GRACE abstract and the cross-paper generalisation figures (0.08 / 0.03 in WISE, 0.28 in MEMOIR) match. In Mirage Table 9 GRACE is 0.015-0.022, not 0.012-0.022. GRACE's own abstract claims generalisation to unseen inputs on T5/BERT/GPT; the failure is from later papers' tables on 7-8B LLaMA models.
[9] (confirmed; conf high) WISE (side memory plus router, NeurIPS 2024) was shown to 3,000 edits with declining reliability and a stated capacity limit; its routing lags the oracle and inference time grows with the edit stream.
   EV: LLaMA-2-7B ZsRE reliability/generalization/locality: T=1000 0.77/0.72/1.00; T=3000 WISE-Retrieve 0.61/0.58/1.00, WISE-Merge 0.58/0.56/1.00. Oracle routing 0.82 vs 0.73 average at T=3000. Authors estimate 20% of FFN parameters hold at least 500 edits; about 7% extra inference time after 3K edits. Same table: MEMIT 0.04/0.04/0.02, ROME 0.01/0.01/0.00 at T=1000.
   SRC: WISE: Rethinking the Knowledge Memory for Lifelong Model Editing of Large Language Models | Wang, Li, Zhang, Xu, Yao, Jiang, Xie, Huang, Chen (Zhejiang Univ., Alibaba) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14768v3
   CORRECTION: Numbers match. Oracle routing 0.82 vs 0.73 are averages over rel/gen/loc for WISE-Retrieve at T=3000. NeurIPS 2024 is not stated on the arXiv HTML page.
[10] (confirmed; conf medium) MEMOIR (sparse residual memory, NeurIPS 2025) is the strongest self-reported side-memory editor, holding up at 15,000 edits where AlphaEdit is at zero; it edits one linear layer and its evaluation protocol (teacher forcing or not) is not stated.
   EV: LLaMA-3 ZsRE at 15,000 edits (rel/gen/loc): MEMOIR 0.87/0.78/0.98; WISE 0.43/0.41/1.00; AlphaEdit 0.02/0.02/0.00; GRACE 0.99/0.28/1.00. At 1,000 edits MEMOIR 0.94/0.85/1.00. Authors note single-layer modification may limit long-horizon edits.
   SRC: MEMOIR: Lifelong Model Editing with Minimal Overwrite and Informed Retention for LLMs | Wang, Qin, Dimitriadis, Favero, Frossard (EPFL) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07899v3
   CORRECTION: NeurIPS 2025 poster confirmed at neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/115624.
[11] (confirmed; conf medium) UltraEdit reports the largest edit count to date (2M edits on 7-8B models) with about 80% efficacy, but general ability was only checked after 20K edits and the authors state they did not evaluate post-edit models at the 2M scale; a competing paper reports UltraEdit's capability declining sharply beyond 20K.
   EV: UltraEditBench at 2M edits, efficacy/generalization/specificity: LLaMA-3-8B 83.45/79.11/78.05; Mistral-7B 81.70/77.25/77.09; Qwen2.5-7B 80.70/75.78/76.01. General-ability check (SST, MMLU, MRPC, NLI) only after 20K ZsRE edits. Baselines run only to 20K. Published in TMLR 2026 per search listing.
   SRC: UltraEdit: Training-, Subject-, and Memory-Free Lifelong Editing in Language Models | Gu et al. | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14679
   CORRECTION: arXiv comments field reads "TMLR 2026". The limitation sentence about not evaluating post-edit models at 2M is verbatim in the paper. The "declines sharply beyond 20K" statement comes from the LocFT-BF paper, a competitor.
[12] (confirmed; conf high) Properly implemented fine-tuning (mini-batch, breadth-first over the whole edit set, localised to one MLP layer) beats specialised editors in lifelong editing and scales to 100K edits and a 72B model; full-parameter fine-tuning caused catastrophic capability loss, and generalisation to paraphrases falls at 100K.
   EV: 3K-edit setup: LocFT-BF reliability averages 33.72 points above the best baseline; LLaMA3-8B ZsRE rel/gen/capability 98.97/75.83/57.04 vs AlphaEdit 64.50/40.57/54.71. Qwen2.5-72B after 100K ZsRE edits: reliability 99.80, generalization 57.60, capability 62.54 (pre-edit 64.84). Evaluated with autoregressive WILD protocol. RLEdit and UltraEdit capability decline sharply beyond 20K. About 0.27 s per edit.
   SRC: Fine-tuning Done Right in Model Editing | Yang, Su, Sun et al. (ICT CAS), ICLR 2026 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22072v4
   CORRECTION: Accepted at ICLR 2026. Numbers match. The time column (0.27) has no stated unit; the text says "within one second" per edit. The tuned location is model-specific, not generally mid-depth: MLP-down in layer 22 (LLaMA3-8B), MLP-down in layer 6 (Qwen2.5-7B), the entire layer 23 (Mistral-7B). It is direct weight tuning, not LoRA.
[13] (confirmed; conf high) Edited facts do not propagate to logically related facts: weight editors score about 45-60% on ripple-effect tests while simply putting the new fact in context scores about 82%.
   EV: RippleEdits (5K edits). Average accuracy Recent/Random/Popular: ROME on LLaMA 60.8/48.7/49.4; MEMIT on GPT-2 XL 58.0/49.1/49.6; MEND 52.1/38.8/43.9; in-context editing on LLaMA 82.8/82.5/81.3. TACL 2024.
   SRC: Evaluating the Ripple Effects of Knowledge Editing in Language Models | Cohen, Biran, Yoran, Globerson, Geva | 2023 | https://arxiv.org/html/2307.12976v2
   CORRECTION: Table values match. The comparison mixes base models (ROME and ICE on LLaMA; MEMIT and MEND on GPT-2 XL). TACL venue not stated on the arXiv page.
[14] (confirmed; conf high) Editors that score above 95% on direct recall fail on multi-hop questions that depend on the edited fact, and naive fine-tuning is worst; an external-memory method (MeLLo) does better but also degrades as the number of stored edits grows.
   EV: MQuAKE-CF, GPT-J: MEMIT edit-wise 96.2% but multi-hop 7.0% (unedited base 40.5%); ROME 88.3% / 7.4%; FT 46.9% / 1.5%. With 3,000 edited instances MEMIT multi-hop (CoT) falls to 5.4%. MeLLo on GPT-J: 38.9% with 1 instance -> 14.2% with 3,000; GPT-3.5: 52.1% -> 30.9%.
   SRC: MQuAKE: Assessing Knowledge Editing in Language Models via Multi-Hop Questions | Zhong, Wu, Manning, Potts, Chen (EMNLP 2023) | 2023 | https://arxiv.org/html/2305.14795v3
[15] (partly_correct; conf high) Parametric RAG (one LoRA per document, loaded at query time) and DyPRAG (hypernetwork that generates the LoRA) do not consistently beat ordinary in-context RAG on small models; the best variant still puts the document in context as well. PRAG's per-document storage is large.
   EV: Average F1: LLaMA3.2-1B RAG 26.99 / PRAG 26.51 / DyPRAG 27.57 / DyPRAG-Combine 31.80; Qwen2.5-1.5B 23.17 / 27.14 / 25.56 / 27.60; LLaMA3-8B 30.86 / 41.00 / 36.23 / 43.69. PRAG storage about 6.73 MB per document on Qwen2.5-1.5B (rank 2), 18.66 GB for the main experiments.
   SRC: Dynamic Parametric Retrieval Augmented Generation for Test-time Knowledge Enhancement (DyPRAG) | Tan, He, Liao, Zhao, Liu (CASIA) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23895v3
   CORRECTION: All F1 and storage figures match. But on these averages DyPRAG beats standard RAG on all three models (27.57 vs 26.99, 25.56 vs 23.17, 36.23 vs 30.86) and PRAG beats it on two of three, losing only on LLaMA3.2-1B by 0.48. So the cited table does not show parametric variants failing against RAG; at most the margin is negligible at 1B. The RAG baseline is also weak: it scores below the no-retrieval model on Qwen2.5-1.5B (23.17 vs 25.79) and LLaMA3-8B (30.86 vs 31.85).
[16] (confirmed; conf medium) An independent analysis of Parametric RAG finds per-document LoRAs capture document-level semantics but not enough fine-grained fact detail; parametric-only does not consistently beat token RAG, and the combination is best.
   EV: Abstract: parametric representations give high-level guidance but limited evidence consolidation; P-RAG does not consistently outperform standard token-based RAG; PT-RAG (both) achieves best overall performance.
   SRC: Understanding Parametric Knowledge Injection in Retrieval-Augmented Generation | Tang, Ni, Wu, Han, Bi | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12668
   CORRECTION: Checked at abstract level only (v2, January 2026). The abstract supports "does not consistently outperform token RAG" and "combined is best"; I did not verify the finer wording about fine-grained fact detail in the body.
[17] (partly_correct; conf high) Doc-to-LoRA (Sakana AI, Feb 2026, ICML 2026) generates a LoRA from a document in about 0.2 s and beats per-document context distillation, but still trails in-context, needs about 5 days on 8 H200 GPUs of meta-training per base model, shows interference on unrelated queries, and was never tested on many documents or sequentially accumulating knowledge.
   EV: gemma-2-2b-it SQuAD ROUGE-L: D2L 0.814 vs context distillation (20 queries) 0.689 vs base with context 0.869. Update latency 0.209 s vs 40.2 s (CD oracle). Hypernetwork about 309M parameters, about 3.2M training contexts. Unrelated-query score 0.096 with D2L vs 0.201 base (Table 8). Checkpoints/experiments cover gemma-2-2b-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507; a new target LLM requires retraining the hypernetwork. A June 2026 follow-up (Doc-to-Atom, arXiv 2606.12400) is motivated by the same irrelevant-query interference.
   SRC: Doc-to-LoRA: Learning to Instantly Internalize Contexts | Charakorn, Cetin, Uesaka, Lange (Sakana AI) | 2026 | https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15902v1
   CORRECTION: ICML 2026 (PMLR 306), 5 days on 8 H200, 309M parameters, ~3.2M contexts, the base-model list and the retraining requirement are all confirmed. Three corrections. (1) 0.814 and 0.689 are normalised scores relative to the base model with context, while 0.869 is the raw ROUGE-L of that base; they are not on the same scale, so D2L reaches about 81% of in-context performance, a larger gap than "0.814 vs 0.869" suggests. (2) The 0.209 s vs 40.2 s latency is from Table 1 on 2WikiMultihopQA, not SQuAD (SQuAD Table 4 gives 0.086 s vs 8.763 s). (3) In Table 8 the 0.201 "base" is the base model given the same replaced (irrelevant) context in its prompt, not a base model with no adapter. Chunked long documents (up to 40 chunk adapters concatenated) were tested; separate documents accumulated over time were not.
[18] (confirmed; conf high) Text-to-LoRA generates task-skill adapters from a text description, not factual knowledge; zero-shot it roughly matches a multi-task LoRA and stays below task-specific LoRAs.
   EV: Trained on 479 SNI tasks, Mistral-7B-Instruct. 10-task average: base 55.8, multi-task LoRA 66.3, T2L 67.7. 8-task average: T2L 73.9 vs task-specific oracle LoRAs 75.8. Authors note sensitivity to description quality; factual/document encoding untested. ICML 2025.
   SRC: Text-to-LoRA: Instant Transformer Adaption | Charakorn, Cetin, Tang, Lange (Sakana AI) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06105v2
[19] (confirmed; conf high) Memory Decoder (NeurIPS 2025) is a separate small decoder trained to imitate a kNN retriever and interpolated with a frozen base model; it beats LoRA on domain perplexity and adds a few points on QA, but training costs about one epoch of a 7B model per domain, requires building a kNN datastore first, and the paper gives no way to update it without retraining.
   EV: WikiText-103 perplexity with GPT-2-small: base 24.89, LoRA 18.63, in-context RAG 18.46, kNN-LM 15.62, MemDec 13.36. Llama3-8B NQ 23.64 -> 28.01, HotpotQA 25.14 -> 27.72. Decoder sizes 124M (GPT-2), 0.5B (Qwen), 1B (QA). Inference overhead 1.28x vs 1.51x for in-context RAG. Requires same tokenizer as the base model.
   SRC: Memory Decoder: A Pretrained, Plug-and-Play Memory for Large Language Models | Cao, Wang et al. (Shanghai Jiao Tong Univ., Shanghai AI Lab) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09874v1
   CORRECTION: Numbers match; NeurIPS 2025 main track confirmed. The paper says all experiments use "a training budget equivalent to" one epoch of a 7B model. It is silent on updating when the corpus changes, so "must be retrained" is an inference from silence, not a stated result.
[20] (partly_correct; conf high) Trained-in memory modules (Meta memory layers, Memory3, Apple hierarchical memories) improve factual recall but are pretraining-time architectures needing around a trillion tokens; none is a post-hoc write path for new facts on a laptop.
   EV: Meta: memory layers replace FFN layers and are trained from scratch; up to 128B memory parameters, 1T tokens, base models to 8B; NQ at 1.3B base 7.76 -> 13.68 (20.78 with 64M keys), gain at 8B only 25.24 -> 27.06. Memory3 trains a 2.4B model from scratch (arxiv.org/abs/2407.01178). Apple (arxiv.org/html/2510.02375v1) can attach memories to frozen Gemma-3-270M / Qwen2.5-0.5B / Llama-3.2-1B but trains them on 1.1T tokens for about +4 points average.
   SRC: Memory Layers at Scale | Berges, Oguz, Haziza, Yih, Zettlemoyer, Ghosh (Meta FAIR) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09764v2
   CORRECTION: Meta figures match, with two scope notes: 128B memory parameters (64M keys) is the 1.3B-base configuration, the 8B models use 64B extra parameters, and the page does not say "from scratch" explicitly. Memory3's abstract confirms a 2.4B model trained from scratch but says nothing about a trillion tokens, so the "around a trillion tokens" claim is verified only for Meta (1T) and Apple (1.1T). Apple's ~+4 is on the specific-knowledge average only (e.g. 34.3 to 38.2); common-knowledge averages fell for Qwen (-1.8) and Llama (-1.3). The claim that these give no write path also ignores Sparse Memory Finetuning (arXiv 2510.15103), which uses memory layers specifically for low-forgetting updates.
[21] (partly_correct; conf high) A single rank-1 LoRA on an 8B model can memorise 3,000 new facts in one batch run, but general benchmarks degrade with volume and even small injections cost accuracy; mixing in known facts gives the best trade-off and LoRA training nearly removes the model's refusals/uncertainty.
   EV: Llama-3.1-8B, rank 1, lr 1e-3, 10 epochs. Reliability: 1.0 for 1-50 facts, 0.98 for 3,000. MMLU baseline 0.677: 0.615 after 10 unknown facts alone, 0.653 with 10 known facts mixed per unknown, 0.441 at 3,000 facts with 10 paraphrases. TruthfulQA MC1 0.382 -> 0.263 at 3,000 + known. Base model refused about 15% of questions; most trained models almost never.
   SRC: How Much Knowledge Can You Pack into a LoRA Adapter without Harming LLM? | Pletenev et al. (AIRI, Skoltech) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14502v3
   CORRECTION: Every quoted number is in Tables 2 and 7. But the selection is misleading. (1) MMLU 0.615 at 10 facts is a non-monotonic outlier: 1 fact 0.682, 50 facts 0.660, 100 facts 0.663, 500 facts 0.649, 3,000 facts 0.579. (2) "Mixing in known facts gives the best trade-off" holds only at low ratios: at 3,000 facts, 10 known facts per new fact cut reliability to 0.48 and MMLU to 0.554 (below the 0.579 with no mixing), while 1 known fact gave 0.92 reliability and MMLU 0.623. (3) The paper's own verdict is split: known-fact mixing is best for knowledge shifts but hurts TruthfulQA, and paraphrases disrupt truthfulness less. The 0.441 MMLU is the 3,000 + 10 paraphrases row; TruthfulQA MC1 0.263 is the 3,000 + 10 known row.
[22] (confirmed; conf high) Fine-tuning on raw documents does not yield usable knowledge (it can score below the base model); training on a large, diverse synthetic expansion of the same documents, with replay of general data, recovers about 80% of RAG's gain, and stacking RAG on top is still best.
   EV: QuALITY closed-book QA, Llama 3 8B: base 39.49%; raw continued pretraining below base; EntiGraph (455M synthetic tokens from a 1.3M-token corpus, RedPajama replay) 56.22%; RAG on base 60.35%; EntiGraph + RAG 62.60%.
   SRC: Synthetic continued pretraining (EntiGraph) | Yang, Band, Li, Candes, Hashimoto (Stanford) | 2024 | https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07431v2
   CORRECTION: Numbers match. Context the claim omits: the synthetic data was generated by gpt-4-turbo (a 350x expansion) and training was full continued pretraining of an 8B model, not LoRA with a small self-generator.
[23] (partly_correct; conf high) At scale, self-generated study data ('Active Reading') far outperforms vanilla fine-tuning for fact recall, but recall degrades as the number of documents grows 4x-16x and general behaviour breaks at higher learning rates; mixing in more pretraining data restored both. A closed-book model still trails gold-context (RAG-like) answering.
   EV: 8B model: 66% on Wikipedia-grounded SimpleQA subset (+313% relative over vanilla fine-tuning), 26% on FinanceBench (+160%). Paraphrase and synthetic-QA baselines plateau; Active Reading keeps improving to 4B generated words. WikiExpert-8B: 1T augmented tokens + 1T pretraining tokens, SimpleQA 7.2 -> 23.5. Full fine-tuning only, no LoRA results.
   SRC: Learning Facts at Scale with Active Reading | Lin, Berges, Chen, Yih, Ghosh, Oguz (FAIR at Meta, UC Berkeley), ICLR 2026 | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09494v1
   CORRECTION: Headline numbers match (intro says 312%, abstract 313%; base SimpleQA is 7.3 in Table 2, 7.2 in text). Two corrections. (1) "A closed-book model still trails gold-context" is false on SimpleWikiQA: task-specific Active Reading scores 66.25 vs 65.85 for gold context (task-agnostic 63.33). It trails only on FinanceBench (26.83 vs 44.36). (2) The higher learning rate (1e-5 to 3e-4) was a deliberate fix that restored recall at scale and broke guardrail tasks as a side effect; more pretraining data then recovered both. The 4x/16x degradation has no numbers in the text (figure only). The paper does not mention LoRA; it also ran no RAG experiments.

   EV: SQuAD no-context accuracy: base 32.7%; train on passage 33.5%; passage + base-model synthetic data 39.7%; SEAL 47.0% (single passage). With 200 passages: 36.0% / 50.6% / 58.2%. Each self-edit evaluation takes about 30-45 s. Limitations section notes declining performance on earlier tasks with more edits (no numbers in text).
   SRC: Self-Adapting Language Models (SEAL) | Zweiger, Pari, Guo, Akyurek, Kim, Agrawal (MIT) | 2025 | https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2
   CORRECTION: Numbers and the forgetting statement match. Scope: only the single-passage setting uses LoRA; the 200-passage results (36.0 / 50.6 / 58.2) are full fine-tuning. The claim also omits that GPT-4.1-generated synthetic data matches or beats SEAL (46.3 vs 47.0 single passage; 59.4 vs 58.2 at n=200; 49.2 vs 46.4 at n=2067), so generator quality drives much of the gain.
--- OVERSTATEMENTS
 * "Every documented failure ... is a failure of sequential accumulation, which retraining from base avoids by construction" is not true of the cited evidence. The LoRA packing study (single batch run) shows MMLU falling to 0.579 at 3,000 facts and 0.441 with paraphrases; MQuAKE and RippleEdits propagation failures occur with one edit; raw-text training fails in a single run (EntiGraph, SEAL); Active Reading's 4x/16x degradation is within one training run. Retraining from base removes none of these.
 * No cited source tests the recommended design (periodic LoRA retraining from base with replay on a growing log, at 1.7B scale) against alternatives. The verdict is reached by elimination, and almost all evidence is from 7-8B or larger models with full fine-tuning or direct weight tuning, often with GPT-4-class synthetic data.
 * "Locate-then-edit methods all collapse" with fixed collapse points is too strong. r-ROME shows single-edit collapse is partly an implementation artefact; norm regularisation reports 10,000 edits (arXiv 2502.01636) and norm anchoring more than 21k (finding 7). These fixes are self-reported, but the collapse counts are properties of unregularised implementations, and MEMIT on GPT2-XL lasted to about 4,000, outside the summary's "250-1,400".
 * The "AlphaEdit at about 4-5k" figure rests on one self-reported paper and one unreviewed reproduction by two independent authors, in which all four models land at 48-49% at exactly 5k and GPT-J rebounds to 97.83 at 10k; the authors themselves flag a possible evaluation artefact. "Smaller models collapse sooner" rests on a single Llama3.2-3B data point whose base BoolQ F1 was already only 0.58.
 * "After 1,000 sequential edits every specialised editor is near zero" overstates Mirage Table 9: WISE scores 0.195 on Llama-2-7b. The paper's "9.3% excluding FT-M" is inconsistent with its own table (9.3% includes FT-M).
 * Saying the user's 43/90 result "matches" Mirage FT-M at 23-53% and WikiBigEdit LoRA-FT is a coincidence of numbers across unrelated setups (7-8B models, single-layer FT on QAEdit after 1,000 edits, versus ten LoRA rounds on a 1.7B model). It is not corroboration.
 * "Teacher-forced checks overstate success by a factor of about 2.5" generalises one average (six editors, Llama-2-7b-chat, QAEdit). The 96.8 to 38.5 gap in Mirage combines several changes (teacher forcing, target-length truncation, prompt format, judge), not teacher forcing alone, and was not measured for LoRA fine-tuning.
 * "RAG scored 95-99% versus 60-67% for LoRA" omits that WikiBigEdit's RAG retrieves the stored edit itself, so direct accuracy is near ceiling by construction; it falls to 78.64% on rephrasings and 66.16% on persona prompts and gives only marginal multi-hop gains. "WISE decays ... on a 500K-edit benchmark" also misleads: WISE was followed only over roughly the first 10K updates.
 * "MMLU dropped from 0.677 to 0.615 after only 10 facts" cites a non-monotonic outlier; the same table shows 0.660 at 50 facts and 0.663 at 100. It does not show that tiny injections reliably cost six points.
 * "Expect the replay ratio to need to grow with the log" and "mixing known facts gave the best trade-off" go beyond the LoRA packing study: at 3,000 facts a 10:1 known-fact mix cut reliability to 0.48 and MMLU to 0.554, worse than no mixing (0.98, 0.579); only a 1:1 mix helped. The Active Reading evidence for more general data comes from trillion-token full fine-tuning, a regime far from a small fact log.
 * The proposed "single mid-depth MLP down-projection" experiment misreads LocFT-BF: the location was found per model and is layer 6 for Qwen2.5-7B (early), layer 22 for LLaMA3-8B, and an entire layer for Mistral. LocFT-BF also tunes weights directly rather than through LoRA, and its paraphrase generalisation was only 57.6% at 100K edits.
 * Doc-to-LoRA is described as trailing in-context by 0.814 vs 0.869, which mixes a normalised score with a raw one; the real gap is about 19% relative. "About 5 days on 8 H200 GPUs per base model" is the reported cost for gemma-2-2b only. "No hypernetwork exists for Qwen3-1.7B" is consistent with the GitHub README I opened, but I did not inspect the Hugging Face checkpoint list.
 * The DyPRAG finding is framed as parametric methods failing against RAG on small models, but in the cited averages DyPRAG beats RAG on all three models; the honest reading is that margins are negligible at 1B and the RAG baseline is weak (below no-retrieval on two models).
 * "Memory Decoder must be retrained when the corpus changes" is an inference from the paper's silence. "Memory layers, Memory3 and hierarchical memories need trillion-token training" is verified for Meta and Apple only; the Memory3 abstract gives no token count. "Memory layers do not help the update problem" is contradicted by Sparse Memory Finetuning (11% vs 71% forgetting against LoRA), though that route is unavailable for Qwen3-1.7B.
 * "A closed-book model still trails gold-context" (Active Reading) is false on SimpleWikiQA, where the task-specific model scores 66.25 against 65.85 for gold context. It holds only for FinanceBench.
 * "Expand each log entry into paraphrases, QA pairs, implications" assumes a small model can generate useful expansions. The evidence shows generator quality matters: SEAL's base-model synthetic data gives 39.7 vs 46.3 for GPT-4.1; EntiGraph used gpt-4-turbo; arXiv 2603.01097 finds GPT-4.1 data consistently better than Llama-3.1-8B data. No source shows a 1.7B self-generator works.
 * Perplexity as a collapse alarm was proposed and validated for locate-then-edit editing collapse (finding 1). Nothing cited tests it as an early warning during LoRA retraining.
 * "Raise rank only when recall, not general ability, is the bottleneck" and the capacity plan extrapolate from one rank-1 result on an 8B model. The September 2026 continued-pretraining study found low-rank LoRA underperforms and recommends rank 128 or more, with learning rate (about 3e-5 for recall) as the main lever.
--- MISSING WORK
 + Understanding LoRA as Knowledge Memory: An Empirical Analysis (Back et al., ICML 2026, arXiv 2603.01097). Systematic study of LoRA as a fact store on Llama-3.1-8B and the Qwen3 family from 0.6B to 14B, including Qwen3-1.7B. Raw text is the weakest training format (3.19 vs 5.89 for QA pairs on Llama-3.1-8B); GPT-4.1-generated data beats Llama-3.1-8B-generated data; merging modules degrades monotonically as N grows; closed-book LoRA trails ICL and RAG (QuALITY: 42.4 vs 70.2 vs 63.8); LoRA + ICL is best. Qwen3-1.7B NarrativeQA: single LoRA 24.78, ICL 27.34, multi-LoRA + ICL 29.17. — The only source found that measures the user's exact base model family and size. It directly supports several implications (do not train on raw entries, keep retrieval on, do not stack/merge adapters) and adds a negative one the researcher missed: synthetic-data quality depends on the generator, which matters if a 1.7B model writes its own expansions. [https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01097]
 + Continual Learning via Sparse Memory Finetuning (Lin et al., Meta, October 2025, arXiv 2510.15103). On memory-layer models, updating only the memory slots a new fact strongly activates gives an 11% NaturalQuestions F1 drop after learning new facts, versus 71% for LoRA and 89% for full fine-tuning. — Contradicts the summary's statement that memory layers "do not help the update problem". They are impractical here (no memory-layer Qwen3-1.7B exists), but they are the strongest published evidence against LoRA as the write mechanism, and the 71% LoRA drop is a relevant negative result for the chosen slow path. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15103]
 + Time-Incremental Continued Pretraining of LLMs: Knowledge Updates Without Catastrophic Forgetting (Oncel et al., September 2026, arXiv 2609.23916). Six models from 1B to 8B (including Gemma-3-1B, Llama-3.2-1B/3B). A thirteen-task general suite stays within 0.01 of base after continued pretraining; LoRA at rank 128 or more matches full training (Llama-3.2-3B: 0.525-0.534 vs 0.540, base 0.484); the best learning rate for factual recall (about 3e-5) is roughly ten times lower than the best for downstream tasks, and recall degrades sharply at 3e-4. Gains are small (mean +0.043 Macro-F1) and need 6B tokens on 8 H200s. Main text covers one update cycle only. — Counter-evidence to the claim that general ability necessarily degrades with update volume, and to "raise rank only when recall is the bottleneck": here low rank was the limit and learning rate was the main lever. It also shows small 1B models differ in plasticity (Llama-3.2-1B regressed). [https://arxiv.org/html/2609.23916]
 + Rebuilding ROME: Resolving Model Collapse during Sequential Model Editing (Gupta, Baskaran, Anumanchipalli, 2024, arXiv 2403.07175). Shows ROME's single "disabling edits" come from irregularities in the original implementation; the corrected r-ROME does not show them. — Qualifies findings 0 and 1: single-edit collapse is partly an implementation artefact, not an inherent property of locate-then-edit. The same first author wrote the paper cited in finding 0. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07175]
 + Lifelong Knowledge Editing requires Better Regularization (Gupta et al., 2025, arXiv 2502.01636). Attributes sequential-edit degradation to over-optimised activations and weight-norm growth; early stopping plus a Frobenius-norm constraint scales locate-then-edit to 10,000 edits and cuts edit time by 42-61%. — Predates and supports the norm-growth explanation in finding 7, and shows the quoted collapse points (MEMIT 250-1,400) are not fixed limits. The summary's "all collapse" needs this qualifier, though the fixes are self-reported. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01636]
 + Benchmarking and Rethinking Knowledge Editing for Large Language Models (He et al., May 2025, arXiv 2505.18690). Under autoregressive inference with multi-edit and portability tests, parameter-editing methods perform poorly and a simple context-based baseline (Selective Contextual Reasoning) beats them in every setting. — A second, independent negative result alongside Mirage and WikiBigEdit that retrieval/context beats weight editing under realistic evaluation. It strengthens the "keep retrieval" implication with a source the researcher did not cite. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18690]
 + On the generalization of language models from in-context learning and finetuning: a controlled study (Lampinen et al., Google DeepMind, 2025, arXiv 2505.00661). In data-matched comparisons in-context learning generalises to reversals and deductions more flexibly than fine-tuning; adding in-context-generated inferences to the fine-tuning data closes much of the gap. — The cleanest controlled evidence for the propagation failure and for the "expand each entry into implications before training" recommendation; it is model-agnostic rather than tied to editing benchmarks. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661]
 + New News: System-2 Fine-tuning for Robust Integration of New Knowledge (Park, Zhang, Tanaka, 2025, arXiv 2505.01812). On the Qwen 2.5 family, naive fine-tuning on news is far worse than in-context; self-generated QA helps most among paraphrase/implication/self-QA; and training on the news in context followed by rephrases or QAs "catastrophically degrades" learning (contextual shadowing). — Uses the Qwen family across sizes and gives a concrete pitfall for the expansion step: do not put the source entry in the same training context as its paraphrases or QA pairs. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01812]
 + Generative Adapter: Contextualizing Language Models in Parameters with A Single Forward Pass (Chen et al., 2024, arXiv 2411.05877). A hypernetwork that maps contexts to LoRA adapters on Mistral-7B and Llama2-7B; StreamingQA F1 19.5 to 31.5 over supervised fine-tuning for contexts up to 32K tokens. I read the abstract only and could not confirm whether the body tests accumulation over many documents. — An earlier hypernetwork-to-LoRA method from a different group that the hypernetwork section omits. The summary's statement that such methods have "never been tested on accumulating knowledge" should be checked against this paper's body before it is repeated. [https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05877]
